What most owners miss

One caregiver can sue for all of them

Under 29 USC 216(b), an employee can bring a collective action for herself and every similarly situated employee who opts in. A held-paycheck practice that touches five caregivers is not five separate small problems. It is one case with five caregivers of wages, doubled or trebled, plus one attorney fee award.

The missing timesheet is the employer's problem, not a defense

Federal regulation 29 CFR Part 516 puts the duty to keep time records on the employer. When the records are missing, the employee's reasonable estimate of hours worked can carry the case. Pay from the schedule of record, reconcile next cycle, and handle the paperwork failure as a discipline issue. Discipline may never touch pay for hours already worked.

A payroll reserve is protection, not law

Keeping a reserve of at least one full payroll cycle is sound practice, and this page recommends it as practice. It is not a legal requirement under federal law or the South Carolina Payment of Wages Act, and no statute cited here sets one. The reserve exists so a billing delay or a remittance gap never becomes the reason a caregiver is paid late. Practice, not law, and labeled that way on purpose.

A handbook rule cannot override the statute

No policy an owner writes can outrank federal or state wage law. A handbook line that says no timesheet, no pay does not change the exposure above. It documents the violation in the owner's own handbook.

Methodology and sources

Every figure is computed from the scenario you entered, under the law you picked. Nothing here uses industry averages. The legal multipliers, penalties, and interest rate come from the statutes below, checked on November 1, 2026. How the pieces are presented, and the practice recommendations on this page, are Dr. Boswell's professional judgment from consulting work with agency owners, labeled that way on purpose.

Federal

29 USC 216(b): an employer who violates the wage provisions is liable for the unpaid wages plus an additional equal amount as liquidated damages, plus attorney fees and costs. The same section authorizes collective actions by one or more employees for similarly situated employees. 29 CFR Part 516: the employer's duty to keep records of hours worked and wages paid.

South Carolina

S.C. Code 41-10-80: subsection (C) allows an employee to recover up to three times the amount of unpaid wages, plus costs and attorney's fees, with the amount within that cap set by the court. Subsection (B) sets a civil penalty of $100 for each failure to pay on a payday. The treble figure on this page is the maximum, labeled up to, never a guaranteed outcome.

New York

Labor Law 198(1-a): an employee who prevails recovers the underpayment plus liquidated damages of 100 percent, plus costs, attorney fees, and prejudgment interest. Labor Law 191(1)(a): manual workers, including home care aides, must be paid weekly and not later than seven calendar days after the week in which wages were earned. Labor Law 193: no deduction from wages is permitted outside its narrow list, and a late or missing timesheet is not on it. Interest is estimated at 9 percent per year under CPLR 5004. One honest limit: a 2025 amendment to 198(1-a) softened liquidated damages for first-time late-pay frequency cases where the wages were eventually paid in full, so a first-time frequency slip that was fully cured can price lower than this page shows.
